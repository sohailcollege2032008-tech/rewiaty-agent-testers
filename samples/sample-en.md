# One: The Jar Without a Label

My father kept the weather in jars, in alphabetical order, because a thing you cannot find is a thing you do not own. Autumn stood on the top shelf, brown as tea left too long. Below it stood Fog, a patient breath rather than a wind, and below that Salt, Pine, Chimney.

I was twelve the year I noticed the empty jar. It sat between Thunder and West, unlabeled, only clean glass where a label had been scraped away. I asked about it while he mended the barometer.

— That one is not empty, he said, without looking up.

— It looks empty.

— Things that look empty are usually the ones doing the most work.

He said this the way he said everything: as if the sentence had waited in his pocket for years. If you wanted to know what my father felt, you looked at the sky.

That night fog came in from the sea and pressed against the windows like a hand against paper. I woke because the house had gone quiet. The barometer had stopped ticking; even the sea had stopped.

I went down barefoot. The jar was on the table with its lid off, and my father stood at the window, speaking to the fog too low to hear. I caught one word. It sounded like my mother's name, but she had been gone six years, and we did not say her name in that house.

— Dad, I said.

He turned, and for a moment he looked at me as if I were the stranger and the fog were his daughter.

— Go back to bed, Mara.

— Why is the jar open?

He looked at the jar, then at me, then at the jar again, and his face rearranged itself the way a beach does after a storm.

— Because tonight it is full, he said.

# Two: What the Fog Remembers

## The Room of Jars

Behind the kitchen was a door I had never opened, and behind it a room of shelves, and on the shelves jars. Some bright as new coins, some dark as old honey, some holding only a thin grey line.

**That was the strangest part:** my father stood in the doorway and did not stop me. All my life he had kept that door locked, and now he stepped aside as if waiting for me to be disappointed.

— Every jar is a day, he said. A day I could not keep any other way.

The labels were in his careful hand, the dates going backward: 1998, 1994, 1989, a gap, 1983, nothing. The years after the gap were the years my mother was alive.

— Where are the years after that?

— You are standing in them.

He pointed behind me. One jar there, larger than the rest, holding no weather but a grey, moving thing that turned slowly. The label said MOTHER, and under it, smaller, *do not open until the light turns green*.

— You wrote that.

— I wrote it the night she died. I thought if I kept her in glass, the fog would not take her.

— And did it?

— The fog took her anyway. The fog takes everything. But it left me the jar, and the jar remembers what I cannot.

> Some people keep photographs. Some people keep letters.
> I kept the wind, because the wind was with her at the end,
> and I thought it might be persuaded to give her back.

***

I do not remember deciding to open it. I remember my hand on the lid, warm, my father saying my name once, not as a warning but as a question. Then fog filled the room, and in it there was a voice.

— Mara, it said. «You have grown... you have grown so much.»

It was her voice, the one that called me in from the garden at dusk. My father sank against the shelf and cried openly, hands in his lap.

— You kept her.

— I kept a jar, he said. There is a difference.

The fog thinned. The jar was empty, clean glass. My father looked at it a long time, then laughed: a small laugh, cracked at the edges, but real.

— All these years, he said, I thought I was keeping her. I was keeping myself from having to miss her.

---

We went out to the cliff at dawn.[^1] The fog was lifting in long strips, the light the color of weak tea, and the sea loud again, ordinary and enormous. He took my hand; it was smaller than I remembered.

— What do we do with the jars now?

— We put them back, he said. But we leave the door open.

And we did. The wind came through the house that winter, and sometimes at night it sounded like someone singing in another room. My father said it was just the wind. I said that was the point.

[^1]: The cliff at dawn: a local phrase for the hour when the fog lifts and the boats see the harbor again. My father called it the only honest hour.

[^2]: *Persuaded*: his word, not mine. He used it about weather, engines, and once a cat, never about people. People, he said, are only waited for.
